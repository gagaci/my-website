## Circumstances

We are living through an AI boom that is touching almost every part of life, and software engineering has been one of the most visibly affected fields.

I have been coding for two years. In 2025, I completed internships at several companies while my skills were still developing. I lacked a strong understanding of system design, architecture, algorithms, and other programming fundamentals.

Then, in 2026, coding agents such as Claude Code and Codex arrived. Everyone was excited and started using them. Twitter was full of claims that we could now work 10x or 20x faster, followed by an endless stream of tech gurus explaining how everything worked.

Our team adopted the trend too. At first, it felt like having a magic wand. Claude could build almost any feature you asked for or dive into a complex bug. As a software engineer, you no longer had to understand every detail. You could simply become the middleman between the AI and the pull request.

A task that might have taken a week before AI could now be finished in minutes.

However, this efficiency comes at a cost. Your understanding of the task and its requirements becomes shallow—if you understand them at all. After a few iterations, you may have no idea what is happening inside the codebase. Your agent starts leading you, when it should be the other way around.

## A Better Way

My point is not that we should abandon AI. We should use it in a way that helps us grow, learn, and gain more experience.

We are not meant to press buttons until a feature is finished. We are meant to understand the feature, question the decisions behind it, and explore its edge cases.

I have started coding manually again. I use AI to review my code, handle repetitive tasks, help me explore unfamiliar parts of a codebase, and write test cases for important features. It remains a powerful tool, but I decide where we are going.

I am leading my AI.
